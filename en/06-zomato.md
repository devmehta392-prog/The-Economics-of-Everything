# Why Zomato Delivers Your Biryani at ₹30 and Still Loses Money

It's 1 pm. You're hungry. You don't want to cook. You open Zomato, browse for five minutes, and order biryani from a restaurant 4 kilometres away.

A delivery partner — a real human being on a real motorcycle — navigates through real city traffic, picks up your order, rides back, and hands you hot food at your door. The whole process takes around 30 minutes. Your delivery fee: ₹30.

At India's minimum wage, 35 minutes of human labour costs more than ₹30. Add fuel. Add vehicle depreciation. Add server costs. Add customer support. Add the marketing budget that got you on the platform. The actual fully-loaded cost of your delivery is almost certainly ₹80–120.

Zomato was not profitable for most of its existence. And yet it is worth over ₹1.5 lakh crore on the stock market. How?

## What Zomato Actually Is (and Isn't)

Zomato is not a delivery company. Domino's Pizza is a delivery company — it owns the product, employs delivery staff, and earns margin by selling pizzas. The entire operation is vertically integrated within one business.

Zomato is a two-sided platform — a marketplace that creates and maintains a connection between two distinct groups (restaurants and customers) and extracts value by facilitating transactions between them. Zomato doesn't make food. Doesn't own restaurants. Doesn't technically employ most delivery partners. Doesn't own any of the food being delivered.

What Zomato owns is something far more valuable: the relationship and transactional pipe between hungry customers and food businesses across India — at a scale no individual restaurant or chain could afford to build independently. In FY2024: 800+ Indian cities, 350,000+ restaurant partners, millions of orders daily.

> **CONCEPT**
>
> **Two-Sided Platform**
>
> A business model serving two distinct user groups simultaneously, where the value for each group increases as the other group grows. Zomato needs restaurants (product) AND customers (demand). More customers attract more restaurants; more restaurants make Zomato more useful to customers; which attracts more customers. Each side reinforces the other in an accelerating cycle called a two-sided network effect.

## Network Effects: The Force That Makes Platforms Worth Billions

A network effect occurs when a product becomes more valuable as more people use it — not just more popular, but more genuinely useful in a measurable economic sense. The telephone: one telephone is worthless. Two can make one call. One million telephones can facilitate 499 billion unique connections. Value grows exponentially, not linearly.

In a city where Zomato has 50 restaurant partners, it's marginally useful. With 500 partners, genuinely useful. With 5,000 partners, effectively essential — more comprehensive than any other way of accessing the city's food options. And as Zomato's customer base grows, more restaurants are compelled to join. As more restaurants join, the platform becomes more valuable to customers. Each side reinforces the other.

This creates winner-takes-most dynamics: the first platform to reach critical mass becomes almost impossible to displace — not because of superior technology or regulation, but because its network value is so much greater than any competitor starting from scratch. Zomato and Swiggy together control over 95% of India's organised food delivery market. Multiple well-funded competitors — Foodpanda, UberEats India, Ola Foods — tried and were forced to exit.

The Foodpanda and UberEats India exits tell an instructive story about network effect competition. Both companies entered the Indian market with enormous global parent companies behind them — Delivery Hero and Uber respectively — with the financial resources to sustain years of losses in pursuit of market share. Both ran aggressive discount campaigns. Both had superior technology in some respects. Neither could displace Zomato and Swiggy because neither could overcome the core problem: in a market where Zomato had restaurant relationships across 800 cities and a consumer base with deeply established ordering habits, starting from scratch required not just matching Zomato's current network but matching the trust, familiarity, and integrated review data that Zomato had accumulated over years. UberEats India eventually sold its India business to Zomato at a significant loss. Foodpanda sold its operations to Ola. The cost of competing against an established network effect in a winner-takes-most market proved prohibitive even for billion-dollar global companies.

> **BY THE NUMBERS**
>
> **Network Effects vs Regular Business Scale**
>
> A traditional business that doubles in size roughly doubles in value. A platform business with strong network effects can grow non-linearly: doubling users can quadruple value because each new user adds value to every existing user. This non-linear value growth is why investors accept years of platform losses — they're betting on exponential value that only critical mass creates.

## Subsidised Growth: Why Losing Money on Purpose Is a Strategy

Zomato's structural per-order losses are not poor financial management. They are one of the most deliberate choices in modern business — subsidised growth — the dominant playbook of the platform economy.

Zomato's most important long-term asset is not technology or restaurant relationships. It is the habitual behaviour of millions of Indian consumers who reflexively open Zomato when hungry. Habits require time and positive reinforcement. Subsidies — impossibly low delivery fees, heavy discounts, coupons — are the tools Zomato used to create the positive frequency required to build habit at scale.

Every rupee 'lost' on subsidies is not operational waste. It is a customer acquisition and habit-formation investment. If acquiring a customer costs ₹2,000 in cumulative subsidies over six months, but that customer generates ₹600 per year in net positive economics for the next 10 years, the investment yields an extraordinary return. Amazon ran this playbook for nearly 20 years. Uber ran it globally. Every major platform business has used some version of this strategy.

There is an additional actor in this economic story who is rarely discussed: the delivery partner. Zomato's delivery fleet is largely composed of gig workers who are not technically employees but independent contractors. They supply their own motorcycles, pay for their own fuel, handle their own vehicle maintenance, and earn on a per-delivery basis. The 'platform' model allows Zomato to treat delivery capacity as a variable cost rather than a fixed one — scaling rider hours up during peak dinner times and down during slow periods without carrying the fixed cost of a full-time employee workforce. This is why food delivery economics in India are structured the way they are: the cost of the 'last mile' that makes delivery seem impossibly cheap is partly borne by gig workers whose earnings per hour, when vehicle costs and idle time are accounted for, are often below what formal employment would provide.

> **ASIDE**
>
> *Imagine telling a restaurant owner in 2010: 'In 14 years, you will give 25% of your revenue to an app that doesn't cook food, doesn't employ your chef, and doesn't own your kitchen. And you will have no realistic choice but to continue.' They would have asked you to leave. Here we are.*

## The Commission Model: How Restaurants Actually Pay

When a restaurant joins Zomato, it pays a commission of 18–30% on every order. On a ₹500 order, the restaurant pays Zomato ₹90–150. Every time. On every order. Regardless of how thin their margins are. And many restaurants also pay separately for advertising placement within the app.

Yet most restaurants continue. Why? Platform dependency — when a business becomes so reliant on a third-party platform for customer access that operating meaningfully without it becomes effectively impossible. Zomato's market dominance means a restaurant absent from it becomes invisible to a massive customer segment. Leaving Zomato doesn't just mean losing some orders — in many neighbourhoods, it means losing the dominant customer acquisition channel.

> **REALITY CHECK**
>
> Real Talk: The next time a restaurant owner seems frustrated about delivery app economics, they're not being unreasonable. Zomato's commission often makes delivery orders economically marginal for the restaurant — while Zomato captures the commission. Consumer gets cheap delivery. Platform captures the economics. Restaurant bears the cost pressure. The menu price on Zomato is often higher than in-restaurant precisely to offset this commission.

## Blinkit and the 10-Minute Economy

In 2022, Zomato acquired Blinkit for ₹4,447 crore, transforming into a quick-commerce platform. Ten-minute grocery delivery uses hyper-local dark stores — small warehouse-style spaces at 1.5–3km intervals, stocked exclusively for rapid order-picking, not customer browsing.

Groceries are higher-frequency purchases — Blinkit orders might average 15–20 per month vs. Zomato food orders at 4–6. Higher frequency means more habit-forming, more data, more revenue opportunities. Packaged goods also have higher and more stable margins than restaurant food.

The dark store model has specific economics that differ fundamentally from traditional grocery retail. A conventional supermarket like DMart or Reliance Smart operates stores of 20,000–50,000 square feet serving thousands of customers daily who browse and self-select. The store must display every product attractively, manage checkout queues, maintain parking, and employ customer-facing staff. A Blinkit dark store is 2,000–3,000 square feet, never visited by customers, staffed by 10–15 pickers who fulfil orders in under 3 minutes, and located in cheaper industrial or semi-commercial real estate rather than premium retail locations. The cost structure per square foot is dramatically lower — and the revenue per order can be comparable because delivery fees and the service premium offset the cost savings required.

Blinkit's competition — Zepto, Swiggy Instamart, BigBasket Now — has made this segment intensely competitive. But Zomato's bet is that the frequency and habit of grocery purchases, combined with cross-selling opportunities between Zomato food delivery and Blinkit grocery, makes the combined business significantly more valuable than either alone. A consumer who orders food on Zomato and groceries on Blinkit three times each per week generates more data, higher engagement, and more monetization opportunities than a consumer who uses either service alone — and the switching cost of moving to a competitor increases with every additional service they use within the Zomato ecosystem.

## From Burning Cash to Making It: The Harvest Phase

In FY2024, Zomato posted its first-ever net profit: ₹351 crore. This wasn't because the business model changed. The investment phase was transitioning into the harvest phase. Once dominant market position was captured, subsidies gradually wound down, commission rates from restaurants maintained, Gold subscriptions providing predictable recurring revenue, new services launching on top of the same user base.

Zomato Gold — the paid membership program offering free deliveries and restaurant discounts — exemplifies the harvest phase strategy perfectly. Early in Zomato's growth, delivery subsidies were broadcast to everyone as the default, training millions of users to expect cheap delivery as a baseline. In the harvest phase, those subsidies are consolidated into a paid membership: ₹299 per month buys you the delivery benefits that used to come free. Members who sign up have self-selected as the highest-frequency, highest-value users — exactly the segment Zomato wants to retain at any cost. The membership fee provides predictable recurring revenue while making the economics of subsidized delivery sustainable within the subscriber segment.

The losses were never losses. They were timed, strategic investments in market capture. The returns are now being collected — precisely as the original strategy intended.

Zomato started by delivering biryani at a loss. It is now building infrastructure that makes it extraordinarily difficult for restaurants, customers, and grocery supply chains to imagine operating without it. That's the real business.

> **THE TAKEAWAY**
>
> What This Means For You: Every time you use a heavily discounted service on any app, ask: who is subsidising this? The answer is almost always 'investors.' Then ask: will I stay when the discounts disappear? Companies like Zomato are betting you will — because habit and convenience are more durable than discounts. They're generally right. The most expensive moment in any app relationship is when the discounts stop — because that's when you find out whether the product itself was ever worth paying full price for.
