# How UPI Changed the Economics of Money — And Why the World Is Copying India

Three seconds. That's how long it takes. Tap your phone. Enter a PIN. ₹250 sent to your friend who paid for lunch. Done.

It works at 2 am on a Sunday. Works on a ₹6,000 Android phone or a ₹1,30,000 iPhone. No bank branch. No cheque. No ATM. No minimum balance. No transaction fee. No waiting period. Just — done.

India processes 13 billion UPI transactions every month. Total value crossing ₹200 lakh crore per year — approximately $2.4 trillion. For comparison: this is more transaction volume than Visa and Mastercard handle combined in most major economies. Running on infrastructure built by a government entity. Available for free to anyone with a phone and a bank account.

> **ASIDE**
>
> *India went from 78% cash-dependent in 2015 to teaching Singapore, France, and UAE how digital payments should work in 2024. This is a sentence that would have seemed like someone's joke in 2015. The country that the world was advising on economic development is now advising the world on payment infrastructure. Economics moves fast when the incentives are right.*

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 2016 | ~0.1 million | UPI launched by NPCI |
| Nov 2016 | Significant spike | Demonetisation forces mass first-time digital adoption |

| 2020 | ~2 billion | COVID-19 accelerates contactless payment preference |

| 2024 | ~13 billion+ | More than most countries' entire payment system volumes combined |

*UPI's extraordinary growth — from near-zero to the world's most-used real-time payment system in 8 years*

## The Problem UPI Solved: Why Your Money Was Trapped

Before UPI, digital payments in India were a fragmented, incompatible mess. HDFC Bank customers could use HDFC's NetBanking. SBI customers used YONO. Paytm users could transfer to other Paytm users. Each ecosystem worked within its own walls — but sending money instantly from your HDFC account to someone's SBI account, from a mobile app, at midnight on a Sunday, with no fees? Genuinely difficult.

The fundamental problem: a lack of interoperability. Your money was trapped within your bank's ecosystem. NPCI solved this by building UPI as shared rails — a single technical standard that all participating banks must connect to. Once connected, any bank account can send to any other bank account, instantly, 24/7, through any UPI-compatible app.

Think of it like the national highway network. Private roads exist on private land. The National Highway is a shared standard everyone connects to — which makes the total road network vastly more valuable than any collection of private roads. UPI is the payment equivalent: shared infrastructure that makes every connected bank more useful by making them all interoperable.

> **CONCEPT**
>
> **Interoperability**
>
> When different systems can communicate and work together seamlessly. UPI made Indian banking interoperable — your money can flow between any two bank accounts, regardless of which banks, through any UPI app. For comparison: the US still struggles with this. Venmo users can only easily send to other Venmo users. PayPal to PayPal. India's UPI solved in 2016 what the world's most sophisticated financial economy still hasn't fully solved.

## Demonetisation: The Painful Catalyst

On November 8, 2016, Prime Minister Modi announced that ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes — representing 86% of all currency in circulation — would cease to be legal tender at midnight. Hundreds of millions of Indians woke up the next morning with currency that was no longer money.

The economic disruption was severe and widely documented. Queues at bank branches stretching hundreds of meters, lasting weeks. Cash ATMs running dry. Daily-wage workers unable to receive payment in valid currency. Informal markets — which ran almost entirely on cash — seizing up overnight.

But one unintended consequence was permanent: a forced, mass-scale experiment in digital payments across every demographic and geography. With cash unavailable, millions who had never used a digital payment — tea stall owners, vegetable vendors, auto-rickshaw drivers, kirana operators, domestic workers — were suddenly strongly motivated to try UPI. Not because they chose to. Because they had no realistic alternative.

What made the demonetisation catalyst particularly powerful was that the adoption it forced happened at the exact moment when smartphone penetration was crossing a critical threshold in India. Reliance Jio had launched in September 2016 — just two months before demonetisation — with effectively free data and dramatically reduced handset prices. India was adding tens of millions of smartphone users per month. UPI landed on hundreds of millions of new phones at precisely the moment when people desperately needed a non-cash payment alternative. The coincidence of these two events — demonetisation and the Jio revolution — created conditions for digital payment adoption that no planned government campaign could have manufactured.

> **ASIDE**
>
> *Demonetisation was simultaneously one of the most economically painful and most effective digital payment onboarding campaigns ever run — entirely unintentionally. The government wanted to eliminate black money. It accidentally created a nation of UPI users. The economists and the marketers are still arguing about which outcome was more significant.*

## Why UPI Is Free: The Economics of Public Goods

For consumer-to-merchant and person-to-person transactions, UPI is completely free. No per-transaction fee. No monthly charge. No percentage of transaction value.

This is genuinely remarkable by global standards. Visa and Mastercard charge merchants 1.5–3% of every transaction. American Express charges more. Wire transfers cost ₹500–2,000. Yet UPI charges nothing.

This was a deliberate policy decision based on a specific economic argument: payment infrastructure is a public good, and public goods create far more social value when free at the point of use than when taxed per transaction. Charging even ₹2 per transaction would disproportionately affect low-income users who transact in smaller amounts more frequently. Zero fees remove this friction entirely — enabling adoption across every income level.

> **BY THE NUMBERS**
>
> **India as the World's Teacher on Payments**
>
> Singapore's PayNow, Brazil's Pix, and the EU's SEPA instant payments all share UPI's open design philosophy. India's G20 presidency in 2023 made digital public infrastructure — with UPI as the centrepiece — a priority agenda item. India offered to share UPI's technical architecture with interested countries through the India Stack platform.

## How PhonePe and Google Pay Make Money on a Free Product

If UPI transactions are free, how do PhonePe (valued at $12 billion+), Google Pay, and Paytm build multi-billion-rupee businesses on top of free payment infrastructure?

They don't make money from the UPI transaction itself. The payment is a loss leader — a free service offered to build a relationship and establish a user habit. The revenue comes from everything built on top of that relationship: financial product distribution (insurance, mutual funds, credit cards) earning distribution commissions; lending to merchants and consumers based on transaction data; commerce platforms; data intelligence.

The payment is the door. The building behind it is the financial services ecosystem. Build the relationship through a compelling free core product, then monetize the relationship through premium services. The identical economic logic to Zomato's cheap delivery fees or early Netflix subscriptions.

The data angle deserves particular emphasis. When PhonePe processes 10 billion transactions per month, it accumulates a transaction history for hundreds of millions of Indians that no bank, no lender, and no financial institution previously had access to at this level of detail. How often does this person spend? On what categories? Does their spending pattern suggest seasonal income (consistent with agricultural or festival-dependent livelihoods)? Does their UPI-linked merchant pattern suggest they run a small business? This data is extraordinarily valuable for financial services targeting — for identifying who should receive a personal loan offer, who qualifies for a merchant overdraft, whose income pattern makes them ideal for a specific insurance product. The 'free' payment app is simultaneously the world's most detailed financial behavior surveillance system for the Indian economy. And the entity that owns that data owns a significant competitive advantage in serving India's enormously underserved financial products market.

| Platform | Revenue from UPI transfers | Actual revenue sources |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| PhonePe | Zero | Insurance distribution, lending, Pincode commerce, financial products |
| Google Pay | Zero | Data intelligence for Google advertising ecosystem, financial products |
| Paytm | Zero | Merchant lending, financial services, payment device distribution |
| NPCI (UPI operator) | Essentially zero | Government-mandated public infrastructure — not profit-driven |

*The paradox of India's biggest payment apps: free core service, multi-billion rupee businesses*

## UPI Goes Global: India's Most Valuable Export

India has signed UPI connectivity agreements with Singapore, UAE, France, Bhutan, Nepal, Mauritius, Sri Lanka, and others. An Indian traveling in London, Dubai, or Singapore can pay at participating merchants using their Indian bank account through UPI. No Forex card. No currency conversion fees. Just scan and pay.

When India's payment infrastructure becomes the standard for cross-border transactions with multiple countries, India gains: reduced transaction costs for its 35 million strong diaspora; reduced dependence on Western card networks like Visa and Mastercard; and significant diplomatic soft power through technological leadership.

The geopolitical significance of UPI's global expansion is worth understanding explicitly. Visa and Mastercard — both American companies — currently process a substantial fraction of the world's digital payment transactions, collecting interchange fees on every transaction and operating under US financial regulations that give US authorities influence over global payment flows. Countries including India, China, Russia, Brazil, and the EU have been developing domestic payment alternatives partly to reduce this structural dependence on US-controlled financial infrastructure. UPI is India's most advanced and most exportable version of this strategy. Every country that adopts UPI-compatible infrastructure for bilateral payments reduces Visa and Mastercard's role in those flows — and increases India's.

For Indian consumers and businesses, the practical benefits are already tangible. The Indian diaspora — 35 million Indians living abroad — annually sends approximately $125 billion in remittances back to India, making India the world's largest remittance recipient. Traditional international remittance services charge 3–5% on transfers. A UPI-to-UPI transfer between compatible countries could reduce this to near zero, keeping thousands of crores annually in the pockets of Indian families rather than in remittance service fees. The economic development impact of cheap cross-border payments for a country like India — where remittances represent 3% of GDP — is genuinely significant.

The country that was 78% cash-dependent in 2015 is now teaching the world how digital money should work. UPI is not just an app. It is a reminder that government investment in open infrastructure can unlock private sector innovation worth many times the infrastructure's cost.

The full picture of UPI's financial inclusion impact is only visible when you look at what it built on top of: the Jan Dhan-Aadhaar-Mobile (JAM) trinity. Jan Dhan Yojana opened approximately 500 million bank accounts for previously unbanked Indians between 2014 and 2024 — the largest financial inclusion drive in history. Aadhaar created a biometric identity layer that allowed instant, paperless KYC verification, removing the bureaucratic barrier that had excluded hundreds of millions from formal financial services. Mobile data infrastructure — dramatically expanded by Jio — gave those accounts a digital access layer at near-zero marginal cost.

UPI then connected these three components into a functional real-time payment system. A woman in rural Rajasthan who received her first Jan Dhan bank account in 2015, verified her identity via Aadhaar, and got affordable mobile internet via Jio in 2016, could by 2018 receive government welfare payments directly into her account (eliminating the leakage from middlemen that had previously diverted substantial fractions of welfare spending), pay the local merchant via UPI without handling cash, and access micro-credit products from fintech companies using her UPI transaction history as a credit assessment proxy. This chain of financial inclusion — from unbanked to fully-fledged digital participant in the formal economy — happened in under five years for hundreds of millions of people. The economic productivity gains from this inclusion are enormous and still compounding.

> **THE TAKEAWAY**
>
> What This Means For You: UPI's story contains the most important lesson in infrastructure economics: good infrastructure is a force multiplier. The investment in building UPI's rails enabled hundreds of thousands of crores in digital commerce, financial inclusion, and economic activity that wouldn't have existed otherwise. When thinking about government spending, the question is never just 'how much does this cost?' It's 'what does this enable, and is the multiplier effect worth the investment?'
