# Why Your Netflix Bill Keeps Going Up — And Why You Never Cancel

Picture this: It's 11 pm. You've had a long day — school, tuitions, maybe a part-time shift. You open Netflix, scroll for twenty minutes, find nothing you actually want to watch, and still don't cancel.

You close the app, keep the subscription, and next month ₹249 disappears from your account again — exactly like it did last month, and the month before, and the twelve months before that.

That specific moment — the moment of almost-cancelling but not — is worth billions of rupees to Netflix. They didn't earn that money because you watched something great. They earned it because you didn't do anything. And they designed the entire experience so that doing nothing is the easiest possible choice.

In India, Netflix's mobile plan jumped nearly 43% between 2021 and 2023. Their standard plan in the US went from $9.99 in 2014 to $22.99 in 2024. They raised prices more than six times in a decade. And they kept growing subscribers through every single price hike.

How does a company raise prices repeatedly and still grow? Why don't people just leave?

> **ASIDE**
>
> *Netflix has a metric called 'browse time' — the minutes users spend scrolling without watching anything. Billions of people globally spend hours per month just... looking at Netflix. Not watching it. Just looking. Netflix still gets paid. This is either a masterclass in product design or a hostage situation. Possibly both.*

## From DVDs to Dominating Your Evenings

Netflix launched in 1997 as a DVD-by-mail service in America. You'd choose a movie online, they'd send you a red envelope, you'd watch it, mail it back. Slow. Physical. But it had one genius feature Blockbuster didn't: no late fees. Blockbuster made a significant portion of its revenue charging customers fines for returning DVDs late. Netflix replaced that anxiety with a flat monthly fee.

This one change trained a generation to think about entertainment differently. Not 'what does this film cost?' but 'I'm already paying anyway, so I might as well watch.' The per-transaction mindset became a subscription mindset — and that shift changed the economics of entertainment permanently.

In 2007, Netflix launched streaming. In 2013, they produced House of Cards — their first original. This single moment changed everything. Before originals, Netflix was a library. After originals, Netflix became the only place to get certain things. That distinction is the difference between a commodity and a moat.

The history of streaming in India tells a fascinating story about how global platforms adapt to local economic realities. When Netflix launched in India in 2016, it priced itself identically to its US service — starting at approximately ₹500–800 per month. The result was predictable: India's price-conscious, internet-nascent market responded with indifference. Netflix's India subscriber count grew slowly while Hotstar — later Disney+ Hotstar, later JioCinema — built hundreds of millions of users at a fraction of Netflix's price by securing IPL streaming rights and offering a genuinely competitive product at ₹499 per year rather than per month. Netflix's response — introducing the mobile-only plan at ₹199/month specifically for India — was an acknowledgement that global pricing doesn't survive contact with the Indian consumer's value calculus.

Netflix isn't really an entertainment company. It's an irreplaceability company. Every show they commission, every feature they build, every price they set is designed to make the answer to 'should I cancel?' feel like 'not yet.'

> **CONCEPT**
>
> **Content Moat**
>
> A 'moat' in business is anything that protects a company from competition. Netflix's moat is its original content — shows and films you cannot find anywhere else in the world. Sacred Games is on Netflix or nowhere. Squid Game is on Netflix or nowhere. The deeper the moat (more beloved originals), the harder it is for competitors to challenge them and the harder it is for you to leave.

## The Subscription Economy: How the World Stopped Owning Things

Before Netflix, buying entertainment was clean: you paid ₹500 for a DVD and owned it permanently. Netflix invented something different — the subscription model: paying not for ownership but for ongoing access. You don't own the shows. You rent access, month after month, indefinitely, at a price that can rise without your explicit agreement.

The math reveals how dramatically this shifts value toward companies. A DVD costs ₹500 once. Netflix at ₹249/month costs ₹2,988 in a year and ₹14,940 over five years — nearly 30 DVDs worth of money for content you don't own and can't keep.

From the company's perspective, the question shifts from 'how much can I charge for this product?' to 'how long can I keep this customer paying?' This is Customer Lifetime Value (CLV) — the total revenue expected from one customer across the entire relationship.

> **CONCEPT**
>
> **Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)**
>
> The total revenue a company expects from one customer over the full relationship. A Netflix subscriber paying ₹249/month for 5 years = ₹14,940 CLV. This is why subscription companies spend heavily acquiring customers and very little keeping them — the model collects automatically as long as you don't cancel. Every price hike increases your CLV automatically, without you agreeing to it.

The CLV model completely changed how technology companies think about spending money. A traditional business justifies marketing spend by asking 'what is the immediate return?' A subscription business asks 'what is the expected lifetime value of each acquired customer, and how much can we profitably spend to acquire them?' If a Netflix subscriber has a CLV of ₹15,000 over five years, Netflix can justify spending ₹2,000 on marketing to acquire that subscriber and still generate significant returns. This explains why Netflix spends billions on advertising, why they offer limited free trials, and why they invest so heavily in content — each rupee spent on acquisition or retention is evaluated against the multi-year revenue stream that follows.

This logic also explains why subscription businesses grow so aggressively at the expense of short-term profitability. Amazon ran massive annual losses for nearly 15 years while building Prime. Spotify has never posted a consistent annual profit. Netflix itself was unprofitable in India for years. Investors tolerated these losses because the CLV math justified it: lose money now acquiring customers who will pay recurring revenue for a decade or more, and the long-run return far exceeds the short-run investment. Understanding CLV is understanding why the modern technology economy looks the way it does — and why short-term losses are often the most rational long-term investment a subscription business can make.

| Revenue model | You pay | Total over 5 years | Company's CLV |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| DVD purchase | ₹500 once | ₹500 total | ₹500 — transaction complete |
| Netflix subscription | ₹249/month | ₹14,940 minimum | ₹14,940 regardless of how much you watch |
| Netflix + price hikes | ₹249 rising to ₹349+ | ₹18,000+ likely | Every hike increases this automatically |

*The CLV math — why every company on Earth wants to become a subscription business*

## Why You're Psychologically Terrible at Cancelling Subscriptions

When did you last actively decide to keep your Netflix subscription? Not when did you last watch something. When did you sit down, consciously evaluate whether ₹249 was worth it, and deliberately choose to continue paying? Probably never. It just continues. The money leaves your account automatically.

This is not a failure of willpower. It is the precisely engineered outcome of status quo bias — the documented human tendency to stick with whatever is already happening. Doing nothing is the default. Changing requires effort, a decision, and mild discomfort — and our brains are extraordinarily good at avoiding all three.

Netflix's cancellation flow — the series of screens you navigate to actually cancel — is deliberately designed with friction. Multiple confirmation screens. Emotional prompts showing shows in your watchlist. Offers to pause instead of cancel. Messages listing upcoming releases in your preferred genres. Every additional screen reduces cancellation completion rates by measurable percentages.

Then there's the sunk cost fallacy. 'I've already paid for eight months. I should at least use it this month before I cancel.' But those eight months are gone regardless of what you decide now. Your decision should only be about whether next month's ₹249 is worth it — not about protecting a past investment that already happened.

> **ASIDE**
>
> *Netflix knows, with statistical precision, the exact moment in the cancellation flow where most people give up and go back to the home screen. They placed a 'pause subscription' button right there. It is doing exactly what it was designed to do. The button is not for your convenience. It is for their retention rate.*

## The Password Crackdown: Manufacturing Scarcity in a Digital World

In 2023, Netflix cracked down on password sharing globally. The internet erupted — memes, think-pieces declaring PR disaster, social media posts announcing mass cancellations. The collective tone was outrage.

Then the next quarter's results came in. Netflix added nearly 6 million new subscribers. Revenue jumped significantly. Stock hit record highs. What the internet had declared a catastrophe became one of the most successful growth initiatives in Netflix's history.

This happened because of a fundamental principle in digital economics: you cannot discover the true market price of something until you consistently charge for it. Before the crackdown, over 100 million households were watching Netflix for free by sharing accounts. Many genuinely wanted Netflix. But the free option was available, so they used the free option. Remove the free option, introduce a reasonably priced alternative, and many of those users discover they will pay.

> **CONCEPT**
>
> **Artificial Scarcity**
>
> Natural scarcity limits supply through physical constraints. Artificial scarcity limits supply through rules and enforcement in products that could theoretically be made available to everyone at no extra cost. Netflix's 'one household per account' rule created artificial scarcity around something infinitely copyable. Manufactured scarcity forces willingness to pay to surface — and it turns out, the willingness was there all along.

## The Ads Tier: A Masterclass in Price Discrimination

In late 2022, Netflix launched something it had publicly promised it would never do: a cheaper subscription tier supported by advertisements. Analysts called it desperation. In reality, it was textbook third-degree price discrimination.

Price discrimination is charging different prices to different customers for essentially the same product, based on their willingness to pay. Netflix identified a massive problem: hundreds of millions of potential subscribers who genuinely wanted Netflix but were unwilling or unable to pay the full price. The ads tier captures them. And the existence of the cheaper tier anchors the premium tier — when ₹149/month exists, ₹649/month feels more premium, more deliberate, more of a statement.

| Tier | India price | Who buys it | How Netflix earns |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Mobile (with ads) | ₹149/month | Students, budget users, price-sensitive families | Subscription + advertising revenue from brands |
| Basic | ₹199/month | Singles, light users | Subscription revenue only |
| Standard | ₹499/month | Couples, families sharing 2 screens | Higher subscription margin |
| Premium | ₹649/month | Quality-focused users, large families | Highest subscription revenue per user |

*Netflix's price discrimination model — same content, completely different economics for each tier*

## Content as Capital: The Economics of Making Originals

When Netflix spends $17 billion on content annually, critics question sustainability. Netflix isn't buying entertainment. It's buying moat — permanent competitive barriers made of beloved stories that only deepen with each season. Squid Game — made with a budget of approximately $21 million — reportedly added 4.38 million new subscribers in a single quarter. One show. One creative decision. A return on investment worth hundreds of millions in annual subscription revenue, extending indefinitely into the future.

> **ASIDE**
>
> *Squid Game cost $21 million to make and generated an estimated $900 million in subscriber value for Netflix. The profit margin on that creative gamble was roughly 4,200%. And all Netflix had to do was say yes to a Korean show that every major Hollywood studio had turned down for 10 years. Timing, luck, and the courage to greenlight something unusual.*

Netflix didn't sell you a subscription. It sold you a collection of unfinished stories and a quiet anxiety about what you might miss if you cancel. In 21st-century economics, there is nothing more valuable than that.

## Why Netflix Will Keep Getting More Expensive (And You'll Probably Stay)

Netflix will raise prices again. This is the logical continuation of a flywheel business model that gets stronger the longer it runs: more subscribers fund more originals; more originals attract more subscribers and make existing ones harder to lose; harder-to-lose subscribers give stronger pricing power; stronger pricing power funds more content. Each revolution of the flywheel makes the system faster and harder to stop.

In the Indian context specifically, Netflix faces an unusually competitive streaming market that shapes its strategy differently than any other geography. India has Disney+ Hotstar — which owns the IPL digital rights and reaches hundreds of millions of viewers — Sony LIV, Amazon Prime Video, ZEE5, and dozens of regional platforms. More crucially, JioCinema and Doordarshan's streaming services offer enormous amounts of content for free or at very low price points. In this environment, Netflix's ₹149 ads-supported tier is not just about capturing budget subscribers globally — it is specifically designed to be competitive in a market where the consumer's reference point for streaming cost is dramatically lower than in the US or Europe.

Netflix India's content strategy reflects this competitive reality. Investments in Hindi originals — Delhi Crime, Scam 1992 (co-produced), Kota Factory, Masaba Masaba — serve to create the same content moat in the Indian market that Squid Game and Stranger Things created globally. The goal is identical: build a library of Indian originals that cannot be watched anywhere else, creating a reason for price-conscious Indian consumers to maintain their subscription even as they compare it against cheaper alternatives. The economics of content moat are geography-independent — the mechanism works the same in Jaipur as in Jakarta.

And the psychological hooks are already set. The unwatched shows in your queue. The new season dropping next month. The friend who keeps referencing something only Netflix has. You might complain about the next price increase. You might switch to the ads tier for a few months. But the probability that you cancel entirely decreases with every show you watch and every recommendation the algorithm gets right.

> **THE TAKEAWAY**
>
> What This Means For You: Before paying for any subscription this month, ask two questions: Have I used this in the last 30 days? Would I pay for it today if I didn't already have it? If the answer to both is no — you're not buying value. You're losing money to status quo bias. Cancel it now, not next month. There is no 'next month.' There is only the automatic renewal that already happened.
