# Why an iPhone Costs ₹1,20,000 — And Why You Still Want One

Walk into any Apple Store. Or just look around your college campus, your office, or the next coffee shop you sit in.

Something is happening that is worth paying attention to. People don't just own iPhones. They identify with them. The white AirPods. The specific camera module shape visible from across the room. The sound of an iMessage notification. The logo visible in every meeting, every video call, every photo.

For this signal, people willingly pay ₹1,20,000. For a phone. A device with roughly ₹25,000–30,000 worth of physical components inside it.

Understanding why requires understanding something much deeper than hardware specifications or brand affection. It requires understanding how Apple built the most powerful economic ecosystem in consumer technology — and why, once you are inside it, leaving costs far more than just the price of a new phone.

## What Apple Is Actually Selling (It's Not a Phone)

Apple sells a closed ecosystem — a tightly integrated world of devices, software, services, and social identity that becomes progressively more valuable the deeper you enter it, and progressively more painful to leave.

Consider what happens the moment an iPhone user decides to switch to Android. They don't just swap phones. They also lose: iMessage group chats and the blue bubble. Their Apple Watch (doesn't meaningfully pair with Android). Their AirPods' intelligent features. Their iCloud photo library. Their App Store purchase history. Years of backups and data. And in many social circles, the green bubble — the visible marker that you've left the Apple world.

> **ASIDE**
>
> *The green bubble. Imagine a piece of technology whose most powerful social statement is not what it does, but what colour it turns other people's text messages. Apple didn't design this as a social hierarchy tool. They just... didn't make it easy to fix. The result is identical. Economics and passive aggression working in perfect harmony.*

> **CONCEPT**
>
> **Ecosystem Lock-In**
>
> A strategy where products work so well together — and so poorly with competitors' products — that switching becomes costly not because any single product is irreplaceable, but because leaving means losing the benefits of all of them simultaneously. Apple Watch only fully works with iPhone. AirPods Pro's features are largely iPhone-only. MacBook's best features require Apple devices on both ends. Each product you add makes leaving all the others more expensive.

## The Cost to Make an iPhone vs. What You Pay

Independent teardown analyses estimate the Bill of Materials for the iPhone 15 Pro — all physical components — at approximately $500 USD, or roughly ₹41,000. The phone retails in India for ₹1,34,900. That is a hardware gross margin of approximately 65–70%.

For context: a typical manufacturing company earns 20–30% gross margin. A premium automobile manufacturer earns 15–25%. Apple earns margins that most industries consider structurally impossible, in a sector — consumer electronics — where fierce competition has made margins razor-thin for most participants.

Apple competes on what its products make you feel and what they say about you — not on what they contain. That competition has no objective ceiling the way hardware specifications do. You can measure processor speed. You cannot measure social belonging. Because there is no ceiling on what people will pay for belonging, there is no ceiling on Apple's pricing power.

> **CONCEPT**
>
> **Veblen Good**
>
> Economist Thorstein Veblen identified products where demand rises when prices rise — because high price is the point. A Rolex at ₹5,000 would not be desirable as a Rolex — it would just be a cheap watch. The status signal requires the premium price. If Apple dropped the iPhone to ₹40,000, some of its appeal would evaporate — not because the product changed but because the signal it sends would change.

## The App Store: A Toll Booth on the Digital Economy

When a developer creates an app and sells it through the App Store, Apple collects 30% of every transaction. Every mobile game, every Netflix subscription initiated on iPhone, every in-app purchase of any kind — Apple takes 30 rupees of every 100 rupees that changes hands within its ecosystem.

Apple's Services segment — App Store commissions, iCloud, Apple Music, Apple TV+, the enormous fees Google pays to be Safari's default search engine — generated $85 billion USD in fiscal 2023. As a standalone business, this would rank among the largest technology companies on the planet. And the gross margins on Services are estimated above 70%, compared to roughly 37% on hardware.

The competitive implications of this commission structure are enormous — and increasingly controversial. Epic Games sued Apple in the US over the 30% cut, arguing it was anti-competitive. Spotify publicly criticised Apple for taking 30% of subscriptions initiated on iPhone, noting that this either forced Spotify to raise its iPhone subscription price above its standard price or absorb a margin hit on every Apple subscriber. The EU's Digital Markets Act now requires Apple to allow alternative app stores in Europe — a regulatory challenge that Apple is navigating carefully because the App Store's revenue contribution to the Services segment is significant.

For Indian consumers, the practical implication is direct. Every app that earns money — from CRED to Paytm to Meesho to Zepto — when accessed through an iPhone in-app purchase, sends 30 rupees of every 100 to Apple. This explains why many Indian apps encourage users to purchase or pay through their website rather than through the in-app mechanism: it's not a technical preference, it's a 30% margin decision. The App Store is a toll booth that every digital business operating in the Indian app economy must either pay or strategically route around.

> **ASIDE**
>
> *Apple earns approximately $20 billion per year just from Google paying to be the default search engine on Safari. Not from their own services. Not from app commissions. Just from letting Google exist in a specific spot on an iPhone screen. This is the most expensive piece of real estate in the history of human commerce — one tiny Safari default setting.*

> **BY THE NUMBERS**
>
> **The App Store's India Revenue**
>
> India's app economy is rapidly growing, and Apple earns a 30% commission on most of it. Every Netflix subscription, Spotify Premium, dating app, game purchase, and productivity app bought through iPhone generates App Store commission. Apple didn't make the app. Didn't sell the service. Didn't do the work. It simply owns the pipe through which all of it flows.

## The EMI Economy: How ₹5,999/Month Feels Different from ₹1,20,000

₹1,20,000 paid today feels enormous. ₹4,999 per month for 24 months feels manageable — roughly the cost of two restaurant dinners per month, a discretionary category that urban Indian professionals accept without deep deliberation.

Economically, these options are identical in total cost. But psychologically, they are completely different experiences. This is temporal discounting — the documented principle that humans value money in the present moment far more intensely than equivalent money in the future. Paying ₹1,20,000 today is a large, visible, immediate sacrifice. Paying ₹4,999 next month is a problem for future-you. And future-you seems surprisingly far away and unreal.

EMI restructures a large present sacrifice into many small future ones that feel individually acceptable. Apple deploys this elegantly in the context of a product people feel emotional about. Apple didn't invent this — consumer lending has exploited temporal discounting for decades across cars, appliances, and real estate. But Apple makes it feel aspirational rather than financial.

| Payment method | Monthly feel | Total cost | Psychological experience |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Full payment (₹1,20,000) | Painful — large immediate sacrifice | ₹1,20,000 | Felt as major loss right now |
| 12-month EMI (no cost) | ₹10,000/month | ₹1,20,000 | Feels like 'just ₹10,000 this month' |
| 24-month EMI (no cost) | ₹5,000/month | ₹1,20,000 | Feels like a monthly subscription |
| 24-month with processing fee | ₹5,200/month | ₹1,24,800 | Still feels cheaper — cognitive illusion |

*Temporal discounting in action — identical money, completely different psychological experience*

## Apple in India: The Aspiration Strategy

Apple executed a sophisticated multi-front India strategy simultaneously: domestic manufacturing through Foxconn and Tata (reducing import duties, providing 'Made in India' positioning), premium retail stores in Mumbai and Delhi (full Apple Store experience identical to New York), zero-cost EMI through major bank partnerships, and a trade-in program reducing effective upfront cost.

In FY2024, Apple's India revenue crossed ₹67,000 crore — a 33%+ year-on-year increase. India became one of Apple's fastest-growing markets globally. The company that once largely ignored India now manufactures here, retails here prominently, and has made India central to its global production and consumer strategy.

The manufacturing decision deserves particular analysis. India's import duty on fully assembled mobile phones is 20%. When Apple manufactured iPhones entirely in China and shipped them to India, it paid this duty on every unit, embedded in the retail price — making iPhones even more expensive relative to US prices. By manufacturing a significant and growing proportion of iPhone production in India through Tata Electronics (which acquired Wistron's India operations) and Foxconn's India facilities, Apple avoids the import duty on India-manufactured units. This is why the 'Made in India' iPhone isn't just a nationalist talking point — it is a direct margin improvement or price reduction that makes iPhones more competitive in the world's fastest-growing large smartphone market.

The Apple Store openings in Mumbai and Delhi in 2023 were not simply retail events. They were brand statements — signals to India's aspirational consumers that Apple takes the Indian market seriously enough to invest in the full premium retail experience it offers in New York, London, and Tokyo. The queues that formed outside the BKC Mumbai store on opening day, the social media coverage, the treated-as-global-news quality of the coverage — all of it reinforced precisely the brand positioning Apple wanted: not a foreign company condescending to offer India a product, but a premium global brand choosing India as a priority market.

## The Ecosystem Trap: When Leaving Costs More Than Staying

The iPhone purchase is not a transaction. It is an enrolment. Once you own an iPhone, a quiet economic logic unfolds. AirPods work best with iPhone — ₹17,000. Apple Watch integrates seamlessly in ways no Android watch can — ₹35,000. MacBook's best features require Apple devices on both ends — ₹1,00,000+.

You now have approximately ₹2,70,000 invested across four product categories in one ecosystem. Each device you added made leaving all the others more expensive than it was with just the phone. Each purchase was individually reasonable. The cumulative lock-in was the product.

This cumulative lock-in dynamic has a specific name in economic strategy: increasing switching costs. Each additional Apple product you acquire doesn't just add the standalone switching cost of that product — it multiplies the switching cost of the entire portfolio. Leaving the iPhone means losing Apple Watch functionality. Leaving Apple Watch means the ₹35,000 investment becomes significantly less valuable. Leaving MacBook means losing Continuity Camera, Handoff, Universal Clipboard, and the dozens of features that require Apple devices on both ends. The switching cost of leaving the ecosystem grows super-linearly with each device added.

Apple understands this dynamic precisely, which is why it invests so heavily in ensuring that its devices work together in ways competitors cannot replicate. The deep integration between iPhone and MacBook — where you can answer phone calls on your MacBook, copy on your iPhone and paste on your MacBook, use your iPhone as a high-quality webcam for your MacBook — is not primarily about convenience. It is primarily about making the combined system worth more than the sum of its parts, so that each device makes all the others harder to replace. This is ecosystem engineering: building interdependence so that the rational response to owning one Apple product is to own more of them, and the rational response to considering leaving is to reconsider.

Apple doesn't make the best smartphone by every measurable specification. It makes the smartphone that is most expensive to stop using. In business, that is worth infinitely more than any performance advantage.

> **THE TAKEAWAY**
>
> What This Means For You: The question is not whether an iPhone is good — it demonstrably is. The question is whether it's worth the premium for your specific situation. If you're deep in Apple's ecosystem already, leaving would cost thousands in lost functionality. If you're starting fresh, run the full calculation: phone cost + likely future ecosystem additions. Calculate the total ecosystem cost, not just the phone cost.
