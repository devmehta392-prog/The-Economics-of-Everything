# Why Gold Prices Never Really Fall in India

Every economics textbook states a fundamental principle with confidence: when the price of something rises, people buy less of it. The law of demand. The demand curve slopes downward. This is presented as close to a law of nature.

Gold in India breaks this rule consistently, comprehensively, and for reasons so deeply structural that it deserves its own chapter in any honest book about Indian economics.

In 2024, gold crossed ₹74,000 per 10 grams. India's consumer price inflation over four decades ran at roughly 6–7% annually. If gold had simply kept pace with general inflation, it would cost approximately ₹18,000 per 10 grams today. It costs ₹74,000. And demand has never been higher.

Indian households collectively hold an estimated 25,000 tonnes of gold, worth approximately ₹150 lakh crore. More gold sits in Indian homes, temples, and bank lockers than in the official national reserves of any government on Earth.

> **ASIDE**
>
> *Your neighbour aunty's Godrej locker may contain more gold than the official reserves of some small countries. She is aware of this. This is the point. The economics of the aunty's locker and the economics of a national treasury are, in this one specific regard, structurally identical.*

## 5,000 Years of Context That No Economics Textbook Includes

For most of recorded Indian history, there were no banks, no stock markets, no government savings bonds, no insurance, no formal social security. If a farmer had a good harvest year and wanted to preserve that value, the options were genuinely limited: store grain (perishable), invest in land (illiquid and disputable), or buy gold.

And across centuries of extraordinary upheaval — Mughal transitions, European colonial extraction, the Bengal Famine, Partition in 1947, multiple currency reforms, the Emergency — gold proved its thesis again and again. Paper money was issued and devalued. Land could be confiscated or disputed for generations. But a woman who owned gold in 1947 could walk across the Punjab border during Partition with her family's wealth intact, in a form that no authority could easily track, confiscate, or inflate away overnight.

When you buy a mutual fund, you are trusting a fund manager, a regulatory system, a stock market, and the continued functioning of financial institutions that have existed for at most a few decades in India. When an Indian woman buys gold jewelry, she is placing trust in something that has maintained purchasing power across 50 generations and multiple civilizational disruptions.

> **CONCEPT**
>
> **Price Inelastic Demand**
>
> Demand is price inelastic when it doesn't decrease significantly as price rises — usually because the product serves an essential or deeply embedded need. Gold in India exhibits extreme price inelasticity: even at ₹74,000 per 10 grams, wedding demand doesn't meaningfully drop because the social and ritual obligation of giving gold at a wedding doesn't negotiate with market prices. When something is inelastic, price hikes don't kill demand — they mainly hurt the buyer's wallet.

## The Wedding Economy: Where Price Is Largely Irrelevant

An estimated 10 million weddings occur in India every year. Gold is not optional at an Indian wedding. It is socially compulsory — embedded in religious ritual, family honor, the legally recognized concept of streedhan (wealth belonging to the bride personally under Indian law), and social expectations that have structured Indian marriage for centuries.

The amount of gold at a wedding is not merely jewelry. It is a public statement made in front of extended family, community elders, and everyone who will discuss the wedding afterward. If gold doubles in price, Indian weddings still happen. The social obligation to give gold does not weaken. Families save more aggressively or borrow against existing assets — but they do not replace gold with a fixed deposit certificate.

> **ASIDE**
>
> *At an Indian wedding, the phrase 'the gold was okay' is a devastating critique of the family's entire financial trajectory. The phrase 'the gold was very good' is passed as verified information across generations. This is not materialism. It is a highly functional social credit scoring system that happens to be worn as jewelry.*

> **BY THE NUMBERS**
>
> **The Wedding Gold Economy**
>
> 10 million weddings annually × conservative 50 grams average = 500 tonnes of gold demand from weddings alone — more than half of India's total annual gold imports. High-end North and South Indian weddings can involve 500–2,000 grams per family. Wedding gold demand is effectively a minimum floor under annual gold consumption that price increases cannot easily breach.

## Gold as India's Informal Insurance System

For hundreds of millions of Indians in rural and semi-urban areas, gold was not a choice among financial alternatives — it was the only accessible alternative for emergency liquidity, store of value, and informal credit.

When a farmer's crop failed and the family needed immediate cash, they couldn't sell mutual fund units or draw on a credit line. They sold gold — at the local jeweller's shop, immediately, at a price universally understood, with no paperwork, no credit score, no waiting period. Muthoot Finance and Manappuram — two massive listed companies — exist entirely because of this dynamic. Their existence and scale is testimony to how deeply embedded gold-as-collateral is in the financial logic of hundreds of millions of Indian families.

Muthoot Finance's numbers illustrate this phenomenon with striking clarity. As of FY2024, Muthoot Finance had gold loan assets under management of approximately ₹85,000 crore — that is ₹85,000 crore worth of gold pledged as collateral for cash loans by Indian households. Manappuram adds another ₹22,000 crore. Together, these two companies alone have facilitated over ₹1 lakh crore in gold-backed lending to millions of Indian households who needed liquidity faster than any bank loan process could provide. And behind them sit hundreds of local and regional gold loan companies operating at the district and taluka level.

The economics of gold loans reveal something fundamental about financial access in India. A formal bank personal loan requires a credit score, employment documents, income proof, possibly a guarantor, and processing time of days to weeks. A gold loan requires one thing: physical gold. You walk in, they weigh it, you receive 75% of its market value in cash within the hour. The entire formal credit assessment apparatus that banks require — credit history, income stability, employment verification — is replaced by a single, universally liquid, universally valued asset that you already own. For populations that have historically been excluded from formal credit — small farmers, daily wage workers, street vendors, domestic workers — gold-backed lending is not an inferior product. It is the only feasible product, and it has been serving this function for centuries before Muthoot or Manappuram formalised it.

## The Inflation Hedge: Why Sophisticated Investors Also Buy Gold

The Indian rupee has lost approximately 85% of its purchasing power over the past 40 years. Gold cannot be inflated — the total amount on Earth is determined by geology, not government policy. Annual global gold mining adds only 1.5–2% to total supply per year — far less than money supply growth in most major economies.

There is also the rupee depreciation factor. Gold is priced in USD internationally. The rupee has depreciated from approximately ₹44 per dollar in 2003 to approximately ₹83 in 2024. Even if international gold prices had stayed completely flat, Indian gold prices would have risen 47% in rupee terms purely from currency depreciation.

To put the inflation hedge argument in concrete terms: a family that bought 10 grams of gold in 2000 for approximately ₹4,400 holds an asset worth approximately ₹74,000 in 2024 — a return of roughly 16.8x over 24 years, or approximately 11.4% compounded annually. Over the same period, India's Sensex delivered approximately 13–15% compounded annual returns. Gold underperformed equities on this comparison — but with dramatically lower volatility, no management fees, no counterparty risk, and no requirement for a demat account, a broker, or any financial literacy beyond 'gold holds value.' For the households that bought this gold — many of whom had no equity exposure whatsoever — the 11.4% compounded return was not only respectable, it was likely the best financial decision available to them given their circumstances and knowledge.

This is the crucial nuance that urban, financially-literate commentators often miss when criticising India's attachment to gold. The alternative was not 'buy diversified equity mutual funds via SIP.' The alternative, for hundreds of millions of households, was 'keep money in a savings account earning 4% before inflation,' 'hold cash at home,' or 'buy land with all its illiquidity and legal complexity.' Evaluated against those realistic alternatives — not against the theoretical best option — gold's track record is genuinely strong.

## Why Government Policy Cannot Beat 5,000 Years of Habit

The Indian government has tried repeatedly to reduce gold imports: import duties raised to 15%, the Gold Monetization Scheme offering interest for depositing physical gold in banks, Sovereign Gold Bonds paying 2.5% annual interest. None meaningfully reduced physical gold demand over any sustained period.

The Gold Monetization Scheme failed because it misunderstood why Indians hold physical gold. It asked households to surrender physical possession — to give their jewelry to a bank — in exchange for paper interest. But physical possession is inseparable from gold's insurance value. Bank failures have happened in Indian memory. Physical gold in your hands cannot fail.

Sovereign Gold Bonds are genuinely excellent financial products. But they cannot replace gold jewelry at a wedding. No bride's family can present 200 grams of Sovereign Gold Bond certificates and have that carry the same social weight as 200 grams of physical jewelry worn by the bride.

The government's frustration with gold demand is understandable from a macroeconomic perspective. India imports approximately 800 tonnes of gold annually, at a cost of approximately $40–45 billion in foreign exchange. This is the second-largest import category after crude oil. The gold import bill contributes meaningfully to India's current account deficit — the gap between what India earns from exports and what it spends on imports. A country that imports $45 billion worth of a metal that generates no industrial output, no employment, and no technology transfer is, from a macroeconomic perspective, exporting capital in exchange for a store of value that could theoretically be served by domestic financial instruments.

Yet every government effort to redirect this demand has failed at the margins. The reason is structural: gold's value proposition in India is not primarily financial. It is social, cultural, historical, and psychological. You cannot replace a 5,000-year-old wealth preservation technology and its embedded cultural practices with a policy circular and a 2.5% interest payment. The government that figures out how to match gold's complete value proposition — portable, private, universally liquid, socially meaningful, historically proven, zero counterparty risk — with a domestic instrument will have accomplished something genuinely unprecedented in financial history.

Gold in India is not irrational. It is the accumulated financial wisdom of a civilization that learned, through centuries of hard experience, to trust what no government can print, devalue, or confiscate overnight.

> **THE TAKEAWAY**
>
> What This Means For You: Gold is not automatically a good investment for you at any price. It earns zero income (no dividend, no interest), has making charges on purchase, and can significantly underperform during periods of rising real interest rates. But before dismissing your parents' attachment to it as superstition, understand what problem it was solving for them in their historical and economic context. The deeper lesson: before calling any economic behaviour 'irrational,' first ask what rational problem it was originally designed to solve.
